What you walk into

Four exercises, three hours, total 10. Spectra is usually the fattest (about 3 points). After January 2019, every multiple choice has exactly one correct circle, and those questions sit inside all four blocks rather than in a separate Q1. January 2019 was the old style: four MC in Q1, one or two letters allowed, −0.25 per extra wrong letter. Ignore that rule unless you sit that paper.

They grade formulation and order. A correct number with no likelihood written is not full marks. Every year you shade a plot or fill a table in detection, often in spectra too.

Aids: one A4, both sides, typed or handwritten, plus a calculator and a dictionary. The 2026–27 study guide does not promise a provided formula sheet. Write your own; see the A4 page.

Block 1 · they swapped the condition

Bayes, with the complement in the denominator

You are given how the data look if a cause is true (sensitivity, pass rate of a machine). You are asked how likely the cause is, given the data. Those are not the same number. Bayes unswaps them. The distractors are always the sensitivity, or \(P(\text{data})\) with no cause.

$$P(B\mid A)=\frac{P(A\mid B)\,P(B)}{P(A\mid B)\,P(B)+P(A\mid B^c)\,P(B^c)}$$

Name the events before you touch a calculator. \(A\) is what you observed. \(B\) is the hidden cause. The second term in the denominator is the whole point: the data can also happen when \(B\) is false.

Practice 2021, virus test. Prevalence 1.8%. Sensitivity 95%. False-positive rate 3%. Probability you are infected given a positive test.

Let \(B=\) infected, \(A=\) positive. Then \(P(B)=0.018\), \(P(B^c)=0.982\), \(P(A\mid B)=0.95\), \(P(A\mid B^c)=0.03\).

$$ P(B\mid A) =\frac{0.95\cdot 0.018}{0.95\cdot 0.018+0.03\cdot 0.982} =\frac{0.0171}{0.0171+0.02946} \approx 0.367 $$

Official: 37%. Why 95% is wrong: in 10 000 people, about 180 are infected and 171 of those test positive. About 9 820 are healthy and 295 of those still test positive. Most of the positives are healthy. Rare disease, noisy test: the false-positive pile wins.

Same skeleton, new story, every recent paper:

  • Jan 2020 paint machines: three machines, given pass rates, asked \(P(\text{machine 3}\mid\text{pass})\). Official \(P(\text{pass})=0.819\), then \(0.18\).
  • Jan 2022 resit employees: official 0.26.
  • Nov 2022 “followed the course”: sometimes they only ask the denominator, i.e. total probability. Derived \(0.65\cdot 0.75+0.35\cdot 0.4=0.6275\approx 0.63\).

Do this one before you look

Three paint machines make 30, 45 and 25 cars a day. Pass rates 0.85, 0.92, 0.60. A random car passed. Probability it came from machine 3?

Official, Jan 2020

Total pass: \((30\cdot 0.85+45\cdot 0.92+25\cdot 0.60)/100=0.819\). Then \(P(M_3\mid A)=0.25\cdot 0.60/0.819=0.18\).

Valid ACF, WSS, ergodic, discrete vs continuous

A random variable is one number. A process is a whole waveform per outcome of the experiment. One recording is one realization, not the process.

Wide-sense stationary: constant mean, and the autocorrelation depends only on the lag, not on absolute time. Ergodic: one infinitely long realization already contains the ensemble (time average = ensemble average, and the variance of that estimator goes to 0). Stationary does not imply ergodic.

The standard pair: \(X(t)=A\) with \(A\) random. Every realization is a horizontal line at a random height. Mean is constant, autocorrelation is constant, so it is WSS. Time average of realization \(i\) is \(a_i\), which is not \(E[A]\). Not ergodic in the mean. You cannot learn the ensemble by watching one resistor forever.

\(X_n=A\cos(\omega_0 n)\) with fixed phase is the other trap. Mean is 0, but \(E[X_{n_1}X_{n_2}]\) still contains \(\cos(\omega_0(n_1+n_2))\). Not WSS.

Valid autocorrelation, three tests in order:

  1. Even: \(r[-\ell]=r[\ell]\) for a real process.
  2. \(r[0]\) is the maximum: \(r[0]\ge|r[\ell]|\).
  3. The DTFT \(P(e^{j\theta})=\sum r[\ell]e^{-j\ell\theta}\) is never negative.

January 2020 1a: four plots. \(r_A\) has \(|r[1]|>r[0]\). \(r_D\) is not even. \(r_C\) is even and \(r[0]\) is the max, but \(P(\pi)=2.5-4+2-1=-0.5\). Official: only \(r_B\). The third test is the one people skip.

Pixel colour 0–255 is a discrete random variable. A whole picture is one realization of a random signal. Phone number, dice count: discrete. Weight, commute time: continuous. January 2019 1a official: B and C (dice count, phone number).

Block 2 · freeze the data, pick \(\theta\)

Maximum likelihood

You never see \(\theta\). You see noisy data that depend on \(\theta\). Freeze the dataset you actually got. The PDF as a function of \(\theta\) is the likelihood. Pick the \(\theta\) that makes this dataset most plausible. In practice: write the joint, take \(\ln\), differentiate, set to 0. If the support of the PDF moves with \(\theta\), do not differentiate. Look at the function.

IID samples: the joint is a product, the log is a sum. Semicolon \(p(x;\theta)\) is classical (no prior). Bar \(p(x\mid\theta)\) is Bayesian. Mixing them is a free point lost.

January 2019 2a, exponential. \(p(x;\lambda)=\lambda e^{-\lambda x}\), \(N\) iid samples. Joint \(\lambda^N\exp(-\lambda\sum x_i)\). Log: \(N\ln\lambda-\lambda\sum x_i\). Derivative: \(N/\lambda-\sum x_i=0\).

$$\hat\lambda_{\mathrm{ML}}=\frac{N}{\sum_i x_i}=\frac{1}{\bar x}$$

Official: that formula. It is “one over the sample mean”, which is what a rate should be. For finite \(N>1\) it is biased (\(E[\hat\lambda]=N\lambda/(N-1)\)). They still want the MLE, not the unbiased cousin.

Rayleigh trap, two exams, two PDFs. November 2019: \(p=(x/\theta^2)\exp(-x^2/(2\theta^2))\) ⇒ MLE has a square root, \(\hat\theta=\sqrt{(1/(2N))\sum x^2}\). Practice 2021: \(p=(x/\theta)\exp(-x^2/(2\theta))\) ⇒ \(\theta\) is already a scale-squared, no square root, \(\hat\theta=(1/(2N))\sum x^2\). Read the exponent before you copy last year’s answer.

November 2022 2d, angle in 2-D Gaussian: maximising the likelihood is maximising \(x_1\cos\theta+x_2\sin\theta\), which peaks at \(\hat\theta_{\mathrm{ML}}=\mathrm{atan2}(x_2,x_1)\). \(\arctan(x_2/x_1)\) loses the quadrant. No official key; that is derived.

Invariance (resit 2022 2b, official c): if \(\alpha=\psi(\theta)\) is one-to-one, \(\hat\theta_{\mathrm{ML}}=\psi^{-1}(\hat\alpha_{\mathrm{ML}})\). You maximise in the convenient parametrization, then transform.

Fisher information and the CRLB

Unbiased estimators cannot be arbitrarily sharp. Fisher information \(\mathcal I(\theta)\) is the expected curvature of the log-likelihood. The Cramér–Rao bound says \(\mathrm{Var}\ge 1/\mathcal I\), not \(\ge\mathcal I\). For \(N\) iid samples, \(\mathcal I_N=N\,i(\theta)\), so the bound shrinks as \(1/N\). Resit 2022 2a official: that linear-in-\(N\) fact is the correct MC.

$$\mathcal I(\theta)=-E\left[\frac{\partial^2\ln p}{\partial\theta^2}\right],\qquad\mathrm{Var}(\hat\theta)\ge\frac1{\mathcal I(\theta)}$$

An unbiased estimator that attains the bound is efficient, hence the MVUE. Attaining the bound is the identity \(\partial\ln p/\partial\theta=\mathcal I(\theta)(g(\mathbf x)-\theta)\). If the score does not factor that way, no finite-sample efficient estimator. You can still quote the bound.

If the support of \(p(x;\theta)\) depends on \(\theta\), the bound does not exist. Resit 2022 2e: \(p=2x/\theta\) on \([0,\theta]\). Official: cannot derive a CRLB. Do not grind Fisher. Uniform\([0,\theta]\) is the same trap; the MLE is \(\max_i x_i\), on the boundary, no derivative.

November 2019 3b, Rayleigh above: \(\mathcal I=4N/\theta^2\), so \(\mathrm{Var}\ge\theta^2/(4N)\). The published key writes \(\hat\theta^2/(4N)\). Wrong object: the bound depends on the true parameter, not on the estimate. Report \(\theta^2/(4N)\).

MMSE is the mean. MAP is the mode.

Classical: \(\theta\) is a fixed unknown. Bayesian: \(\theta\) is drawn from a prior, you update to a posterior \(p(\theta\mid x)\), then you pick one number from that posterior. Square cost → posterior mean (MMSE). Hit-or-miss / uniform cost → posterior mode (MAP). They are different functions unless the posterior is symmetric (Gaussian: mean = mode).

Resit 2022 2d official: “MMSE is the mean of the posterior.” “MMSE is the maximum of the posterior” is MAP, and it is the trap.

November 2022 2f–g, posterior handed to you (no official key). \(p(\theta\mid x)=(x+a)^2\theta e^{-(x+a)\theta}\) for \(\theta\ge 0\). That is Gamma, shape 2, rate \(b=x+a\). Mean of Gamma(shape, rate) is shape/rate. Mode of Gamma(2, \(b\)) is \(1/b\).

$$\hat\theta_{\mathrm{MMSE}}=\frac{2}{x+a},\qquad\hat\theta_{\mathrm{MAP}}=\frac{1}{x+a}$$

They differ because the posterior is skewed. When the paper gives you \(p(\theta\mid x)\), do not re-derive Bayes. Read mean vs mode off the given density.

Resit 2022 2f–h, BPSK in Gaussian noise. \(Y=X+N\), \(X=\pm 1\) equally likely, \(N\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2)\). Posterior mean of a discrete \(\pm 1\) is \(P(X=1\mid y)-P(X=-1\mid y)\). After cancelling exponentials that is \((e^{2y/\sigma^2}-1)/(e^{2y/\sigma^2}+1)\). The paper prints \(\tanh u=(e^{2u}-1)/(e^{2u}+1)\). Official:

$$\hat X_{\mathrm{MMSE}}=\tanh(y/\sigma^2),\qquad\hat X_{\mathrm{MAP}}=\mathrm{sign}(y)$$

MMSE is a soft number in \((-1,1)\). MAP is a hard decision at 0. Same sign, different magnitude. The \(\tanh\) identity is usually printed; still write the two-Gaussian posterior first (that is 2f).

Uniform prior on \([0,2]\) (January 2019 2c): MAP is the MLE projected onto \([0,2]\). The published key writes only \(N/\sum x_i\) and skips the projection. If the unconstrained MLE sits outside, the maximum is the endpoint.

No noise PDF → least squares

January 2020 Q4. Signal \(s[n]=A e^{\phi n}\), noise PDF not given. Official: least squares, “requires no function for the noise.” If \(\phi\) is known the model is linear in \(A\):

$$\hat A_{\mathrm{LS}}=\frac{\sum x[n]e^{\phi n}}{\sum e^{2\phi n}}$$

Both unknown: \(J=\sum(x[n]-A e^{\phi n})^2\), nonlinear. Iterate. Gauss–Newton linearises the signal and takes a linear least-squares step. Newton–Raphson iterates on \(J\) itself with the Hessian. Resit 2022 2c: both are legal. The 2020 key printed the Newton form even though the question said Gauss–Newton. Write whichever they named; know the difference.

Geometry, one sentence: the residual \(\mathbf x-\mathbf H\hat\theta\) is orthogonal to the columns of \(\mathbf H\). \(\hat\theta\) itself lives in that span, it is not orthogonal to it. That MC is a free point if you remember which vector is which.

Block 3 · three curves on \([-\pi,\pi]\)

This is about 3 of 10 points. Typical open part: they give you an autocorrelation, or a difference equation. You plot a correlogram, you fit AR(1) with Yule–Walker, you overlay an MA, you quote values at \(0,\pi/2,\pi\). There is also one MC on periodogram / Bartlett / Welch / windows.

Correlogram from a given ACF

$$P_{\mathrm{cor}}(e^{j\theta})=r[0]+2\sum_{k\ge 1}r[k]\cos(k\theta)$$

Only use the lags the model allows. A true MA(\(q\)) has \(r[k]=0\) for \(|k|>q\), so the correlogram of those lags is the MA spectrum.

November 2019 2a. \(r[0]=1\), \(r[\pm 1]=0.7\), \(r[\pm 2]=0.5\), \(r[\pm 3]=0.3\), else 0. Official:

$$P_{\mathrm{cor}}=1+1.4\cos\theta+\cos 2\theta+0.6\cos 3\theta$$

At \(\theta=0\) that is 4. The ACF was truncated, so this cosine polynomial can go negative (official sketch: small negative sidelobes). An AR fit of the same lags cannot: the AR spectrum is forced positive. Label the axis \([-\pi,\pi]\) and quote min/max at \(0\) and \(\pi\).

AR(\(p\)) Yule–Walker

Course convention: \(x[n]+a_1 x[n-1]+\cdots=i[n]\), so the denominator is \(|1+a_1 e^{-j\theta}+\cdots|^2\). Getting that sign wrong wrecks the PSD.

$$a_1=-\frac{r[1]}{r[0]},\qquad\sigma_i^2=r[0]+a_1 r[1],\qquad P_{\mathrm{AR}}=\frac{\sigma_i^2}{|1+a_1 e^{-j\theta}|^2}$$

Negative \(r[1]\) ⇒ \(a_1>0\) ⇒ high-pass (dip at 0, more power at \(\pi\)). Practice 2021 3b official: \(a_1=1/7\), \(\sigma_i^2=64/63\), \(P(0)=0.78\), \(P(\pi)=1.38\). The paper’s fallback ACF flips the sign of \(r[1]\) on purpose. If you missed 3a, the fallback draws the wrong shape.

The 2×2 Yule–Walker right-hand side is \((\sigma_i^2,0)^\top\), not \((r[1],r[2])\). AR(2) is the same idea with a 3×3 Toeplitz (November 2019 2b, official \(0.5089/|1-0.6863 e^{-j\theta}-0.0196 e^{-j2\theta}|^2\)).

MA(\(q\)), and min-phase

For MA(1): \(r[0]=\sigma_i^2(1+b^2)\), \(r[1]=\sigma_i^2 b\). Quadratic in \(b\), two real roots, reciprocals. Both give the same \(|H(e^{j\theta})|^2\). Keep the min-phase one: \(|b|<1\) (zero inside the unit circle). January 2020 3b: \(b=1/2\), not \(b=2\). The MA spectrum equals the correlogram of lags \(\{0,\pm 1\}\).

Fallback \((a,b)\) printed on the 2022 resit (\(a=1/4,b=5/4\)) is not the true MA(2) and is not min-phase. They put it there so a wrong 3b still lets you do 3c.

ARMA(1,1) from a difference equation

Practice 2021 Q3 and November 2022 3c–g. Recipe:

  1. Read \(a_1,b_1\) from \(H(z)\) or from the DE. Watch the course sign: \(x[n]=i[n]+b_1 i[n-1]-a_1 x[n-1]\).
  2. Impulse response: \(h[0]=1\), \(h[1]=b_1-a_1\) by polynomial division.
  3. Expand \(E[x[k]x[k-\tau]]\) at lags 0 and 1 (or modified Yule–Walker with those \(h[k]\)). That is \(r[0],r[1]\).
  4. For \(|\tau|>1\) the MA part is dead: \(r[\tau]=-a_1 r[\tau-1]\) (AR tail).
  5. Fake AR(1): Yule–Walker on \((r[0],r[1])\) only. Fake MA(1): correlogram of lags \(\{0,\pm 1\}\) only. Sketch both on top of the true ARMA.

Practice 2021 official: \(r[0]=28/27\), \(r[1]=-4/27\), then AR \(a_1=1/7\), \(\sigma_i^2=64/63\), MA correlogram \(28/27-(8/27)\cos\theta\).

The nonparametric MC

Periodogram \(\frac1N|\sum x[n]e^{-jn\theta}|^2\): Fourier transform of the biased ACF, asymptotically unbiased, variance \(\propto P^2\), does not fall with \(N\), inconsistent. A longer record is not a quieter periodogram.

Bartlett: average \(K\) periodograms of non-overlapping length-\(M\) blocks. Variance \(\propto 1/K\). Bias goes up (shorter blocks, wider main lobe). January 2019 1c official: that bias statement is true.

Welch / WOSA: segments overlap. Variance is not \(1/K\). Course view at the same \(K\): variance larger than Bartlett, still below the raw periodogram. January 2020 1b official: D, that fact. “Welch variance decreases as \(1/K\)” is the yearly false statement.

Main lobe = resolution. Side lobes = leakage. A tapered window of the same \(N\) has a wider main lobe than rectangular, so worse resolution, less leakage. Zero-padding interpolates the same smeared curve on a finer grid. It does not raise resolution. January 2019 1c: zeropad-increases-resolution is false.

Blackman–Tukey (lag-window the ACF, then Fourier): consistent if the lag window grows slower than \(N\).

FTD / DFT picture (resit 2022 3a, official B = 1B, 2D, 3C, 4A): infinite cosine → two impulses; window → sinc; DFT = samples of that sinc; zeropad = denser samples, same envelope. Option (a) on that paper has a typo (two “3”s).

Spectral factorisation

Write \(P\) in \(\cos\theta\), replace \(\cos\theta=(z+z^{-1})/2\), factor \(P(z)=\sigma_i^2 L(z)L(z^{-1})\), put every pole and every zero of \(L\) inside the unit circle, scale so \(l[0]=1\). \(L(z^{-1})\) is max-phase and is the trap. Resit 2022 1d official: c, \(L(z)=(1-\frac14 z^{-1})/(1+\frac34 z^{-1})\), \(\sigma_i^2=2\) not \(1/2\). The key notes a possible sign typo in the printed denominator; still pick c.

Correlogram, from memory

\(r[0]=1/21\), \(r[\pm 1]=2/105\), else 0. Write \(P(e^{j\theta})\) in \(\cos\theta\). What process is this, and which MA(1) coefficient do you keep?

Official, Jan 2020 3a–b

\(P=\frac4{105}\cos\theta+\frac1{21}\). It is MA(1), and the correlogram is the MA spectrum. Min-phase \(b=1/2\), not \(b=2\).

Block 4 · which region is “decide \(\mathcal H_1\)”

Estimation outputs a number. Detection outputs a label. The two densities overlap, so every rule makes mistakes. You always write the likelihood ratio \(L=p(x\mid\mathcal H_1)/p(x\mid\mathcal H_0)\) and compare it to a threshold. After algebra you test a scalar. Which side of that scalar is \(\mathcal H_1\) is not always the right tail. That is the exam.

$$\mathcal L=\frac{p(x\mid\mathcal H_1)}{p(x\mid\mathcal H_0)}\gtrless\gamma$$

January 2019 published this ratio upside down. Later papers and SLT7 use \(p_1/p_0\). Write the standard ratio. Flag the 2019 key if you quote it.

H1 heavier tails or larger variance: decide H1 outside the dashed lines. False alarm is the H0 density on that outside. Miss is the H1 density in the middle.

H1 faster exponential or narrower Laplace: mass near zero, so decide H1 for small \(x\). The inequality on \(\sum T(x_n)\) flips when you multiply by −1. Hint on January 2020: “design so \(P_D>P_{\mathrm{FA}}\)” means take this left side.

Shade, always, after you know the region:

  • \(P_{\mathrm{FA}}\): \(\mathcal H_0\) density on the \(\mathcal H_1\)-decision side.
  • \(P_D\): \(\mathcal H_1\) density on the \(\mathcal H_1\)-decision side.
  • \(P_M=1-P_D\): \(\mathcal H_1\) density on the \(\mathcal H_0\)-decision side.

False alarm is not “the overlap”. It is not under \(\mathcal H_1\).

Neyman–Pearson vs Bayes

Neyman–Pearson: maximise \(P_D\) at a fixed \(P_{\mathrm{FA}}=\alpha\). Set the threshold from the \(\mathcal H_0\) law only. The \(\mathcal H_1\) law is not used until you compute \(P_D\).

Bayes, 0-1 costs: \(\gamma=P_0/P_1\). That is not \(\mathcal L=1\) unless the priors are equal. November 2022 4f plots the two weighted PDFs \(P_i\,p(y\mid\mathcal H_i)\); the threshold is where they cross.

ROC: \(P_D\) vs \(P_{\mathrm{FA}}\), upper triangle, through (0,0) and (1,1). Larger separation (bigger mean shift, bigger \(\lambda_1/\lambda_0\)) lifts the curve toward the top-left. If your curve sits in the lower triangle you used the CDF (left tail) instead of the complementary CDF, or you swapped the hypotheses. Practice 2021 1e: “H0 variance too high” cannot flip the ROC into the lower triangle. Official a.

Four PDF pairs you will actually see

Mean-shift Gaussians. \(\mathcal H_0:\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2)\), \(\mathcal H_1:\mathcal N(A,\sigma^2)\), \(A>0\). Decide \(\mathcal H_1\) on the right tail of \(\bar x\). \(P(y>y_{\mathrm{th}})=\tfrac12[1-\mathrm{erf}((y_{\mathrm{th}}-\mu)/(\sigma\sqrt 2))]\). \(P_{\mathrm{FA}}\) uses \(\mu=0\). Tables of \(\mathrm{erf}\) or \(Q\) are given; do not copy one onto the A4.

January 2019 3b, first row: threshold 1, \(\mathcal H_0=\mathcal N(0,1)\), \(\mathcal H_1=\mathcal N(1,1)\). \(Z=1\), \(\mathrm{erf}(1/\sqrt 2)=0.683\), \(P_{\mathrm{FA}}=\tfrac12(1-0.683)=0.159\). Under \(\mathcal H_1\) the threshold sits on the mean, so \(P_D=0.5\). Official table: 0.5 / 0.159; 0.977 / 0.159; 0.842 / 0.159; 0.842 / 0.023. \(P_{\mathrm{FA}}\) does not depend on \(\mu_1\).

Equal-mean, unequal-variance (November 2019 Q4). \(\sigma_1>\sigma_0\). Statistic \(\sum x[n]^2\). H1 is the wide one, so H1 = outer tails. Official at \(x_{\mathrm{th}}=\sigma_1=3\sigma_0\): \(P_D=0.318\), \(P_{\mathrm{FA}}=0.004\). Log-LRT:

$$\ln\frac{\sigma_0^N}{\sigma_1^N}-\frac{\sum x^2}{2\sigma_1^2}+\frac{\sum x^2}{2\sigma_0^2}\gtrless\ln\gamma$$

Two exponentials (January 2020 Q5). \(\lambda_1>\lambda_0\). H1 is the faster decay, mass near 0, decide H1 for small \(x\). \(\mathcal L=1\) gives \(x_{\mathrm{th}}=\ln(\lambda_1/\lambda_0)/(\lambda_1-\lambda_0)\). Official \(\lambda_1=2\), \(\lambda_0=0.1\): \(x_{\mathrm{th}}=1.58\). Then \(P_D=1-e^{-\lambda_1 x_{\mathrm{th}}}\) (left of threshold). Same paper 5b, official: \(P_{\mathrm{FA}}=0.1\Rightarrow x_{\mathrm{th}}=1.05\), then \(P_D=0.9\Rightarrow\lambda_1=2.19\).

Gauss vs Laplace, or two Laplaces. Heavier tails → H1 outside. Narrower Laplace → H1 in the centre, inequality flips. Resit 2022 4b–c official: Laplace vs \(\mathcal N(0,1)\), H1 = tails, \(y_{\mathrm{th}}=\pm 1.5884\), \(P_D=0.4519\), \(P_{\mathrm{FA}}=0.1118\) (uses \(Q(1.59)=0.0559\)). November 2022 4c two Laplaces, derived: \(\sum|x_n|\gtrless_{\mathcal H_1}^{\mathcal H_0}\gamma'\), H1 = small sum.

Matched filter MC (resit 2022 4a, official a): output SNR is maximised. Impulse response is \(s[-n]\) or \(s[N-1-n]\), not \(s[n]\), unless you correlate instead of convolve. It exists for coloured noise too, after whitening.

Which tail?

One sample. \(\mathcal H_0:\mathcal N(0,\sigma_0^2)\), \(\mathcal H_1:\mathcal N(0,\sigma_1^2)\) with \(\sigma_1>\sigma_0\). Thresholds where \(\mathcal L=1\). Where do you decide \(\mathcal H_1\)? What do you shade for \(P_{\mathrm{FA}}\)?

Official, Nov 2019 4a

H1 is wider → decide H1 in the outer tails. \(P_{\mathrm{FA}}\) is the two tails of the narrow H0 Gaussian outside \(\pm x_{\mathrm{th}}\). \(P_D\) is the two tails of the wide H1. Statistic for \(N\) samples: \(\sum x[n]^2\).

Key errata

These are the published keys being wrong, not you.

Jan 2019 3a: the published LRT is inverted, \(\mathcal L=p(\mathcal H_0)/p(\mathcal H_1)\). Later papers use \(p_1/p_0\). Write the standard ratio; flag the key if you quote 2019.

Nov 2019 1a: exam-with-answers says B; oldexams says no option is correct (B is the sequence lagged / reversed). Direct \(r_{xy}[\tau]=\frac1N\sum x[n]y[n+\tau]\) matches shifted-B. Prefer oldexams.

Jan 2019 2b: published MMSE is the one-sample formula despite \(N\) in 2a. The \(N\)-sample version is a truncated \(\mathrm{Gamma}(N+1,S)\), \(S=\sum x_i\).

Nov 2019 3b: the key writes \(\mathrm{VAR}(\hat\theta)\ge\hat\theta^2/(4N)\). It plugged the estimate. The bound is \(\theta^2/(4N)\).

Jan 2019 2c: MAP omits the projection onto \([0,2]\). Uniform prior is flat on \((0,2)\), so MAP = MLE only if the unconstrained MLE sits inside; otherwise \(\hat\lambda=2\).
