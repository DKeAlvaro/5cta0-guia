Block 1 · they swapped the condition

Notation for this block

Every Block-1 Bayes question uses the same five objects. Write them down before you touch a number:

ObjectSymbolExample (virus question)
Observation\(A\)test is positive
Hidden cause\(B\)person is infected
Prior\(P(B)\)1.8% infected, before any test
Likelihood\(P(A\mid B)\)95% sensitivity
Evidence\(P(A)\)positives overall — the denominator

Reading rules. \(P(A\mid B)\) is "probability of \(A\) given \(B\)" — the bar means "given", the right side is what you assume true. \(B^c\) means "not \(B\)". The two conditionals \(P(A\mid B)\) and \(P(B\mid A)\) run in opposite directions and are different numbers: the first is what the test manufacturer prints, the second is what the doctor wants. Bayes turns one into the other. Swapping them is the exam.

Bayes, with the complement in the denominator

You are given how the data look if a cause is true (sensitivity, pass rate of a machine). You are asked how likely the cause is, given the data. Those are not the same number. Bayes unswaps them. The distractors are always the sensitivity, or \(P(\text{data})\) with no cause.

$$P(B\mid A)=\frac{P(A\mid B)\,P(B)}{P(A\mid B)\,P(B)+P(A\mid B^c)\,P(B^c)}$$

Method, every time — write these four lines on the script:

  1. Name. One line: "let \(A =\) [what you observed], \(B =\) [hidden cause]".
  2. List. Prior \(P(B)\); likelihood \(P(A\mid B)\); false-positive rate \(P(A\mid B^c)\). Translate words to symbols first — half the errors happen here, not in the arithmetic.
  3. Denominator. Total probability: the data happen with the cause plus without it. Both terms. This line alone is often worth 1 pt (machines Q2a, Nov22 1a).
  4. Divide. Numerator = the "with the cause" pile; denominator = everything.

Distractor map: the option equal to the sensitivity skipped the denominator. The option equal to the prior ignored the test. If your answer equals either printed number, you fell for it.

Practice exam · 28 Oct 2021 · 1a · 0.5 pt · one correct

A virus has infected 1.8% of a population. A test detects this virus 95% of the time when it is actually present, but it returns a false positive 3% of the time when the virus is not present. If a person selected at random from this population tests positive for the virus, what is the probability that this person is actually infected? Please round to the nearest percent.

  • a) 26%
  • b) 37%
  • c) 95%
  • d) 92%

Step 1 — name. \(A =\) "tests positive" (observed). \(B =\) "infected" (hidden). You want \(P(B\mid A)\), not \(P(A\mid B)\).

Step 2 — list. "Infected 1.8%" is the prior: \(P(B)=0.018\), so \(P(B^c)=0.982\). "Detects 95% when present" is the sensitivity: \(P(A\mid B)=0.95\). "False positive 3% when not present": \(P(A\mid B^c)=0.03\). Note the 95% and the 3% condition on opposite causes — they live on different rows, never add them.

Step 3 — denominator. Positives come from two piles:

$$ P(A)=\underbrace{0.95\cdot 0.018}_{\text{sick, detected}}+\underbrace{0.03\cdot 0.982}_{\text{healthy, false alarm}}=0.0171+0.02946=0.04656. $$

Step 4 — divide.

$$ P(B\mid A)=\frac{0.0171}{0.04656}\approx 0.367. $$

Official: b) 37%. Option c is the sensitivity \(P(A\mid B)\) — step 2 without steps 3–4. Sanity in people, not decimals: in 10 000 people, about 180 are infected and 171 of those test positive. About 9 820 are healthy and 295 of those still test positive. Most of the positives are healthy — rare disease, noisy test, the false-positive pile wins. Whenever the prior is tiny, expect the posterior far below the sensitivity.

Written exam · 20 Jan 2020 · Q2 · 2 pt

A car production company has three machines \(M_1\), \(M_2\), and \(M_3\) for painting cars. It has been observed that 85% of the cars painted by \(M_1\) pass the quality test, while for machines \(M_2\) and \(M_3\) the percentages of a car passing the quality test are 92% and 60%, respectively. Each day, machine \(M_1\) paints 30 cars, \(M_2\) paints 45 cars, and \(M_3\) paints 25 cars. All the cars are mixed at random before quality control.

  • (a) What is the probability that a car will pass the quality test? [1 pt]
  • (b) What is the probability that a car that has passed the test was painted by machine \(M_3\)? [1 pt]

Step 1 — name. \(A =\) "passes". Causes \(M_1,M_2,M_3\) (three, not two — the denominator gets three terms).

Step 2 — priors from counts. 30 + 45 + 25 = 100 cars a day, so \(P(M_1)=0.30\), \(P(M_2)=0.45\), \(P(M_3)=0.25\). Likelihoods: \(P(A\mid M_1)=0.85\), \(P(A\mid M_2)=0.92\), \(P(A\mid M_3)=0.60\). ("85% of cars painted by M1 pass" conditions on the machine: \(P(A\mid M_1)\), not \(P(M_1\mid A)\).)

(a) is step 3 of the method — total probability:

$$P(A)=0.30\cdot 0.85+0.45\cdot 0.92+0.25\cdot 0.60=0.255+0.414+0.150=0.819.$$

(b) is step 4 — and it reuses (a). Never recompute the denominator:

$$P(M_3\mid A)=\frac{0.25\cdot 0.60}{0.819}=\frac{0.150}{0.819}\approx 0.18.$$

Official: \(0.819\) and \(0.18\). Exam tactic: (a) and (b) are one Bayes split across two subparts — (b) without (a) is impossible, so if you blank on (a), write the denominator formula anyway for method marks.

Resit · 24 Jan 2022 (same as resit 24 Jan 2020) · 1a · 0.3 pt · one correct

A small manufacturing company has rated 75% of its employees as satisfactory (\(S\)) and 25% as unsatisfactory (\(S^c\)). Personnel records show that 80% of the satisfactory workers had previous work experience (\(E\)) in the job they are now doing, while 15% of the unsatisfactory workers had no work experience (\(E^c\)) in the job they are now doing. If a person who has had previous work experience is hired, what is the approximate empirical probability that this person will be an unsatisfactory employee?

  • a) 0.24
  • b) 0.25
  • c) 0.26
  • d) 0.28

Step 1 — name. Observed \(E =\) "has experience". Hidden \(S^c =\) "unsatisfactory". You want \(P(S^c\mid E)\).

Step 2 — translate, carefully. "80% of satisfactory had experience" is straight: \(P(E\mid S)=0.80\). But "15% of unsatisfactory had no experience" is \(P(E^c\mid S^c)=0.15\) — the complement of what the formula needs. Convert immediately:

$$P(E\mid S^c)=1-P(E^c\mid S^c)=1-0.15=0.85.$$

Priors: \(P(S)=0.75\), \(P(S^c)=0.25\). Rule: whenever the paper says "had no X" / "did not", flip it to the positive event before listing — plugging 0.15 in as \(P(E\mid S^c)\) is the designed trap.

Steps 3–4.

$$ P(E)=0.80\cdot 0.75+0.85\cdot 0.25=0.600+0.2125=0.8125, \qquad P(S^c\mid E)=\frac{0.85\cdot 0.25}{0.8125}=0.2615\approx 0.26. $$

Official: c) 0.26. Option b is the prior \(P(S^c)\) — ignoring the evidence entirely.

Valid ACF, WSS, ergodic, discrete vs continuous

Words first — they are the MC options. A random variable is one number. A random process is a whole waveform per outcome of the experiment. One recording is one realization — one picture is one realization, not the process. Wide-sense stationary (WSS): constant mean, and autocorrelation depending only on the lag, not on absolute time. Ergodic: one infinitely long realization already contains the ensemble — time average equals ensemble average and the variance of that time-average estimator goes to 0. Stationary does not imply ergodic. The lecture counterexample is \(X(t)=A\) with \(A\) random: WSS, but not ergodic in the mean, because each realization is a different flat line and no single line learns the ensemble mean.

Notation. Autocorrelation at lag \(l\): \(r[l]=E[x[n]\,x[n-l]]\). So \(r[0]=E[x^2]\) is the average power, and \(r[1]\) is how much a sample resembles its neighbour. On a stem plot the horizontal axis is the lag \(l\); the center stem (\(l=0\)) is the power.

Three tests, in order, each with its why:

  1. Even. \(r[-l]=E[x[n]x[n+l]]=r[l]\) — shift the time index and it is the same expectation. For a real process the plot must mirror. No mirror: dead on sight.
  2. Max at zero. \(|r[l]|\le r[0]\) (Cauchy–Schwarz: a signal resembles a shifted copy of itself at most as much as it resembles itself). The center stem must be the tallest. Any taller side stem: dead.
  3. Spectrum never negative. \(P(e^{j\theta})=\sum_l r[l]e^{-jl\theta}\ge 0\) — it is power per frequency, and negative power is impossible. Hand check at \(\theta=\pi\): \(e^{-jl\pi}=(-1)^l\), and \(\pm l\) pair up, so \(P(\pi)=r[0]-2r[1]+2r[2]-2r[3]+\cdots\). If that alternating sum is negative: dead.

Apply in order, stop at the first failure — that is the fastest path through six options.

Written exam · 20 Jan 2020 · 1a · 0.25 pt · one correct

Looking at Figure 1, which sequences are legitimate auto-correlation functions?

  • A. Only \(r_B[l]\).
  • B. Only \(r_D[l]\).
  • C. \(r_A[l]\) and \(r_B[l]\).
  • D. \(r_B[l]\) and \(r_D[l]\).
  • E. \(r_C[l]\) and \(r_D[l]\).
  • F. All.

\(r_D\): fails test 1. Not a mirror — invalid before any arithmetic. That kills B, D, E immediately.

\(r_A\): passes test 1, fails test 2. Even, but a side stem is taller than the center (\(|r[1]|>r[0]\)). This is the option that punishes "even is enough" — checking the mirror and stopping. That kills C and F.

\(r_C\): passes tests 1–2, fails test 3. Even, center tallest — so the first two tests say yes. Read the stems (\(r[0]=2.5\), \(r[1]=2\), \(r[2]=1\), \(r[3]=0.5\)) and run the \(\theta=\pi\) check:

$$P(\pi)=2.5-2\cdot 2+2\cdot 1-2\cdot 0.5=2.5-4+2-1=-0.5<0.$$

Negative power: impossible, not a valid ACF. This is the option that punishes "two tests are enough".

Let \(X\) be a random variable. Which \(X\) defined below is discrete?

  • A. Take a random student in the classroom and let \(X\) be his/her weight.
  • B. Roll the dice \(n\) times and let \(X\) be the number of 4s.

Block 2 · freeze the data, pick \(\theta\)

Notation for this block

ObjectSymbolMeaning
Parameter\(\theta\) (or \(\lambda, A\))classical: a fixed unknown number — not random, not a function of the data
Data\(\mathbf{x}=(x_0,\dots,x_{N-1})\)random: what you observed
Likelihood\(p(\mathbf{x};\theta)\)the PDF as a function of \(\theta\) with the data frozen. Semicolon = classical, no prior
Log-likelihood\(\ln p(\mathbf{x};\theta)\)what you differentiate (log is monotone, same maximizer)
Score\(\partial\ln p/\partial\theta\)its zero is the likelihood equation
MLE\(\hat\theta_{\mathrm{ML}}\)the \(\theta\) that maximizes the likelihood — a recipe in the data, hence random

Bar vs semicolon: \(p(\mathbf{x};\theta)\) is classical (no prior exists). \(p(\mathbf{x}\mid\theta)\) is Bayesian (a prior \(p(\theta)\) exists, next section). Same formula, different claim about \(\theta\). Mixing them on the script is a free point lost.

The word "support" means where the PDF is nonzero (e.g. \(x\ge 0\)). If the support moves with \(\theta\) (e.g. \([0,\theta]\)), the maximizer sits on the boundary and the derivative is not zero — look at the function instead. If the support is fixed, differentiate.

Maximum likelihood

You never see \(\theta\). You see noisy data that depend on \(\theta\). Freeze the dataset you actually got. The PDF as a function of \(\theta\) is the likelihood. Pick the \(\theta\) that makes this dataset most plausible.

Method, every time — four lines on the script:

  1. Joint. IID \(\Rightarrow\) product: \(p(\mathbf{x};\theta)=\prod_n p(x_n;\theta)\). Write it — they mark formulation.
  2. Log. Product \(\Rightarrow\) sum. Drop constants that do not depend on \(\theta\).
  3. Score = 0. Differentiate in \(\theta\) (the data are frozen numbers), set to 0, solve.
  4. Max check. Second derivative < 0 (interior max), or boundary argument if the support moves with \(\theta\).

Sanity per family: a rate is one over a mean time (\(\lambda=1/\bar x\)); a scale of \(x^2\) comes with a square root. If your answer has the wrong shape, re-derive — do not box it.

Retake · 21 Jan 2019 · 2a · 1 pt

For the random variable \(x\) with distribution

$$p(x;\lambda)=\lambda e^{-\lambda x}$$

find the maximum likelihood estimator for \(\lambda\), given \(N\) independent identically distributed observations \(x_i\), \(i\in\{0:N-1\}\).

Step 1 — joint. IID, so the joint is a product. Each sample contributes one \(\lambda e^{-\lambda x_i}\):

$$p(\mathbf{x};\lambda)=\prod_{i=0}^{N-1}\lambda e^{-\lambda x_i}=\lambda^N\exp\Big(-\lambda\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}x_i\Big).$$

Step 2 — log. Products become sums. Drop nothing yet (both terms depend on \(\lambda\)):

$$\ln p(\mathbf{x};\lambda)=N\ln\lambda-\lambda\sum_i x_i.$$

Step 3 — score = 0. Differentiate in \(\lambda\) (the data \(\sum x_i\) is a frozen number here):

$$\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda}\ln p=\frac{N}{\lambda}-\sum_i x_i\stackrel{!}{=}0 \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad \hat\lambda_{\mathrm{ML}}=\frac{N}{\sum_i x_i}=\frac{1}{\bar x}.$$

Step 4 — max check. Second derivative \(-N/\lambda^2<0\), so it is a maximum, and it is interior (\(\lambda>0\), support \(x\ge 0\) does not move with \(\lambda\)).

Official: that formula. Sanity holds (rate = 1/mean). For finite \(N>1\) it is biased (\(E[\hat\lambda]=N\lambda/(N-1)\), because \(1/\sum x_i\) is inverse-gamma) — they still want the MLE, not the unbiased cousin \((N-1)/\sum x_i\). What loses the point here is writing the joint as a sum, or differentiating \(e^{-\lambda x}\) without the \(N\ln\lambda\) term.

Written exam · 4 Nov 2019 · 3a · 1 pt

The data \(x[0],x[1],\dots,x[N-1]\) are observed, where \(x[n]\)’s are independent and identically distributed according to the PDF

$$p(x;\theta)=\frac{x}{\theta^2}\exp\left[-\frac{x^2}{2\theta^2}\right],\qquad x\ge 0.$$

Find the maximum likelihood estimator for the parameter \(\theta\).

Step 1–2 — log-likelihood, term by term. Take \(\ln\) of one sample: \(\ln x-2\ln\theta-x^2/(2\theta^2)\). Sum over \(N\) iid samples. The \(\sum\ln x[n]\) pile does not depend on \(\theta\) so it dies on differentiation — but write it, they mark formulation:

$$\ln p(\mathbf{x};\theta)=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\ln x[n]-2N\ln\theta-\frac{1}{2\theta^2}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}x^2[n].$$

Step 3 — score. Differentiate in \(\theta\) (watch the chain rule on \(1/\theta^2\)):

$$\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\ln p=-\frac{2N}{\theta}+\frac{1}{\theta^3}\sum_n x^2[n]\stackrel{!}{=}0.$$

Solve. Multiply by \(\theta^3\) (\(\theta>0\)): \(-2N\theta^2+\sum x^2[n]=0\), so

$$\hat\theta_{\mathrm{ML}}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}x^2[n]}.$$

Step 4. Keep the positive root (\(\theta>0\) is a scale). Second derivative at the solution is negative — interior max, support \(x\ge 0\) fixed.

Official: that square root. Sanity holds (\(\theta^2\) sits under a mean square). Now the cousin below — same family, different parametrization. Read the exponent before you copy last year's answer.

Final exam · 7 Nov 2022 · 2d · 0.5 pt · no official key

Consider the observation

$$\mathbf x=A\begin{bmatrix}\cos\theta\\\sin\theta\end{bmatrix}+\mathbf w,$$

where \(\mathbf w\) is white Gaussian noise. The probability density function of the observation is

$$p(\mathbf x;\theta)=\frac{1}{2\pi\sigma^2}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\Big[(x_1-A\cos\theta)^2+(x_2-A\sin\theta)^2\Big]\right).$$

Find the maximum likelihood estimator of the parameter \(\theta\).

Step 1 — isolate the \(\theta\) piece. \(A>0\), \(\sigma^2\) known, so the prefactor is a constant. Expand the square:

$$(x_1-A\cos\theta)^2+(x_2-A\sin\theta)^2=x_1^2+x_2^2+A^2-2A(x_1\cos\theta+x_2\sin\theta).$$

Only the last term depends on \(\theta\). Maximising the likelihood \(\Leftrightarrow\) minimising that quadratic \(\Leftrightarrow\) maximising \(x_1\cos\theta+x_2\sin\theta\). Everything else is a constant — cross it out mentally.

Step 2 — phasor form. Any \(a\cos\theta+b\sin\theta\) is \(R\cos(\theta-\phi)\) with \(R=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}\), \(\phi=\mathrm{atan2}(b,a)\). Here \(a=x_1\), \(b=x_2\):

$$x_1\cos\theta+x_2\sin\theta=R\cos(\theta-\phi),\qquad R=\sqrt{x_1^2+x_2^2},\ \phi=\mathrm{atan2}(x_2,x_1).$$

Step 3 — peak. \(\cos\) peaks at 0, so \(\hat\theta_{\mathrm{ML}}=\phi=\mathrm{atan2}(x_2,x_1)\). derived

Trap — why not \(\arctan\). Setting \(\partial/\partial\theta=0\) gives \(\tan\theta=x_2/x_1\), which has two solutions \(\phi\) and \(\phi+\pi\) (max and min). \(\arctan(x_2/x_1)\) returns one number in \((-\pi/2,\pi/2]\) and loses the quadrant: e.g. \((x_1,x_2)=(-1,-1)\) is angle \(-3\pi/4\), but \(\arctan(1)=\pi/4\) — off by \(\pi\). Two-argument \(\mathrm{atan2}\) keeps the signs of \(x_1,x_2\). On the script, the \(R\cos(\theta-\phi)\) line is the line they mark; it proves you picked the max, not just a stationary point.

Resit · 24 Jan 2022 · 2b · 0.3 pt · one correct

Which of the following statements about the maximum likelihood estimator is true?

  • (a) The maximum likelihood estimate is the value of \(x\) that maximizes the likelihood function.
  • (b) For large sample sizes, the maximum likelihood estimate is uniformly distributed, i.e. \(\hat\theta_{\mathrm{ML}}\sim\mathrm{Uniform}(-a,a)\) where \(a=\mathcal I^{-1}(\theta)\).
  • (c) For a transformed parameter \(\alpha=\psi(\theta)\), where \(\psi(\cdot)\) is a one-to-one mapping, the maximum likelihood estimate \(\hat\theta_{\mathrm{ML}}\) can be obtained as \(\hat\theta_{\mathrm{ML}}=\psi^{-1}(\hat\alpha_{\mathrm{ML}})\) where \(\psi^{-1}(\cdot)\) is the inverse mapping of \(\psi(\cdot)\) and \(\hat\alpha_{\mathrm{ML}}\) is the maximum likelihood estimate of the transformed parameter \(\alpha\), respectively.

Three claims, three verdicts. (a) maximises in the wrong variable: the likelihood is maximised over \(\theta\) with the data frozen, not over \(x\). (b) wrong law: asymptotically the MLE is Gaussian \(\mathcal{N}(\theta,\mathcal{I}^{-1})\), not uniform. (c) is invariance — maximise in the convenient parametrization, then transform. Official: c. Fisher information and the CRLB

Notation. Fisher information \(\mathcal{I}(\theta)\) is the expected curvature of the log-likelihood — how sharply the likelihood peaks at the truth. Sharp peak = informative data = low bound. Two forms, same number:

$$\mathcal{I}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\frac{\partial\ln p}{\partial\theta}\right)^2\right]=-\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\partial^2\ln p}{\partial\theta^2}\right].$$

The second is almost always easier: differentiate twice, take expectation, flip sign. \(\mathcal{I}(\theta)\ge 0\) — Fisher is a positive number. (The expected second derivative itself is negative; the 2022 2a trap calls Fisher "strictly negative".) The Cramér–Rao bound: every unbiased estimator has

$$\mathrm{Var}[\hat\theta]\ge\frac{1}{\mathcal{I}(\theta)}\qquad\text{(not }\ge\mathcal{I}\text{ — the reciprocal).}$$

For \(N\) iid samples Fisher adds: \(\mathcal{I}_N=N\cdot i(\theta)\), so the bound shrinks as \(1/N\). An unbiased estimator that attains the bound is efficient, hence the MVUE. The attainment test is the identity \(\partial\ln p/\partial\theta=\mathcal{I}(\theta)(g(\mathbf{x})-\theta)\): if the score factors that way, \(g\) is efficient; if not, no finite-sample efficient estimator exists (you still quote the bound).

Regularity — read before grinding. The proof passes \(d/d\theta\) under the integral, which needs the support fixed. If you see \(x\in[0,\theta]\) or \(x\ge\theta\): stop. No CRLB, MLE is \(\max\)/\(\min\) on the boundary (previous section's uniform). Writing Fisher there earns zero.

MMSE is the mean. MAP is the mode.

Notation shift — the bar appears. Classical: \(\theta\) fixed, \(p(\mathbf{x};\theta)\) with a semicolon. Bayesian: \(\theta\) is drawn from a prior \(p(\theta)\), the same formula becomes a likelihood \(p(\mathbf{x}\mid\theta)\) with a bar, and you update:

$$p(\theta\mid\mathbf{x})=\frac{p(\mathbf{x}\mid\theta)\,p(\theta)}{p(\mathbf{x})}.$$

The denominator does not depend on \(\theta\) — drop it for optimisation. From the posterior you return one number. Which one depends on the cost: square cost → posterior mean (MMSE, \(\mathbb{E}[\theta\mid\mathbf{x}]\)); hit-or-miss/uniform cost → posterior mode (MAP, \(\arg\max p(\theta\mid\mathbf{x})\)). Different functions — unless the posterior is symmetric (Gaussian: mean = median = mode, all three coincide).

Method. MMSE: integrate (or sum, if \(\theta\) discrete) \(\theta\) against the posterior. MAP: differentiate \(\ln p(\mathbf{x}\mid\theta)+\ln p(\theta)\); if the unconstrained peak falls outside the prior's support, the max is the boundary (project the MLE onto the support).

The nonparametric MC

Periodogram \(\frac1N|\sum x[n]e^{-jn\theta}|^2\): Fourier transform of the biased ACF, asymptotically unbiased, variance \(\propto P^2\), does not fall with \(N\), inconsistent. A longer record is not a quieter periodogram. Bartlett: average \(K\) periodograms of non-overlapping length-\(M\) blocks, variance \(\propto 1/K\), bias up. Block 4 · which region is “decide \(\mathcal H_1\)”

Estimation outputs a number. Detection outputs a label. The two densities overlap, so every rule makes mistakes. You always write the likelihood ratio \(L=p(x\mid\mathcal H_1)/p(x\mid\mathcal H_0)\) and compare it to a threshold. After algebra you test a scalar. Which side of that scalar is \(\mathcal H_1\) is not always the right tail. That is the exam.

$$\mathcal L=\frac{p(x\mid\mathcal H_1)}{p(x\mid\mathcal H_0)}\gtrless\gamma$$

January 2019 published this ratio upside down. Later papers and SLT7 use \(p_1/p_0\). Write the standard ratio. Flag the 2019 key if you quote it.

H1 heavier tails or larger variance: decide H1 outside the dashed lines. False alarm is the H0 density on that outside. Miss is the H1 density in the middle.

H1 faster exponential or narrower Laplace: mass near zero, so decide H1 for small \(x\). The inequality on \(\sum T(x_n)\) flips when you multiply by −1. Hint on January 2020: “design so \(P_D>P_{\mathrm{FA}}\)” means take this left side.

Shade, always, after you know the region:

  • \(P_{\mathrm{FA}}\): \(\mathcal H_0\) density on the \(\mathcal H_1\)-decision side.
  • \(P_D\): \(\mathcal H_1\) density on the \(\mathcal H_1\)-decision side.
  • \(P_M=1-P_D\): \(\mathcal H_1\) density on the \(\mathcal H_0\)-decision side.

False alarm is not “the overlap”. It is not under \(\mathcal H_1\).

Neyman–Pearson vs Bayes

Neyman–Pearson: maximise \(P_D\) at a fixed \(P_{\mathrm{FA}}=\alpha\). Set the threshold from the \(\mathcal H_0\) law only. The \(\mathcal H_1\) law is not used until you compute \(P_D\). Bayes, 0-1 costs: \(\gamma=P_0/P_1\). That is not \(\mathcal L=1\) unless the priors are equal.

ROC: \(P_D\) vs \(P_{\mathrm{FA}}\), upper triangle, through (0,0) and (1,1). Larger separation lifts the curve toward the top-left. If your curve sits in the lower triangle you used the CDF (left tail) instead of the complementary CDF, or you swapped the hypotheses.
